# Proposal Writing

A proposal does two jobs at once: win the work, and define it precisely enough
that delivering it is still profitable. Documents that do only the first are how
service businesses lose money on won deals.

## Step 1 — Establish what you are actually responding to

Before writing:
- What problem did the client state, in their words? Quote it back later.
- What does success look like **to them**, and how will they measure it?
- Who decides, who influences, and who signs? A proposal aimed at the wrong reader fails.
- Budget range and decision timeline, if obtainable.
- Are you competing, and against whom?

If the problem is not clear enough to write a definition of done, ask before
writing. A proposal built on a guessed problem produces scope disputes later.

## Step 2 — Structure

1. **The problem, in their words.** Show you listened before you sell.
2. **The outcome** you will deliver — stated as a result, not a list of activities.
3. **Approach** — phases, with what happens in each. Enough detail for confidence, not a full method dump.
4. **Deliverables** — a concrete, countable list. "A report" is a dispute waiting to happen; "one 10–15 page written report plus a 60-minute walkthrough" is not.
5. **Out of scope** — explicit. **This is the highest-value section in the document.** Everything you can imagine them assuming is included, and isn't, goes here.
6. **Timeline** with client-side dependencies named (their reviews, their data, their approvals).
7. **What we need from you** — access, contacts, decisions, and by when.
8. **Investment** — pricing, structured as options where possible.
9. **Terms summary** — payment schedule, change-order process, validity period.
10. **Next step** — one clear action.

## Step 3 — Price it as options

Present 2–3 tiers rather than a single number. It shifts the conversation from
"yes or no" to "which one", and it gives the client a way to say yes at a
smaller size instead of saying no.

Get the price from `pricing-and-margin` — proposal pricing set by feel is the
main source of unprofitable jobs. Confirm the loaded cost and target margin
before writing any figure.

## Step 4 — Protect the margin

- **Change-order clause**: work outside the deliverables list is quoted separately before it starts. State it plainly; it is not adversarial, it is how both sides avoid a surprise.
- **Assumptions list**: everything the estimate depends on (client responds within N days, data arrives in format X, one round of revisions included).
- **Revision limit**: name a number. "Until you're happy" is unbounded.
- **Validity period** on the price, typically 30 days.
- **Payment schedule**: deposit up front, milestones thereafter.

## Step 5 — Verify before sending

- Is every deliverable countable and dated?
- Does the out-of-scope section exist and actually contain the likely assumptions?
- Does the price reflect a real cost build-up, at target margin?
- Are client-side dependencies stated, so a slipped timeline is not automatically your fault?
- Do the terms match what `contract-review` would accept? For a material engagement, route it there before sending.
- Are the client's name, entity, and the problem statement correct? A recycled proposal with the previous client's name in it loses the deal.

## Step 6 — Deliver

The proposal (via `docx` or `pptx`), a short internal note on the margin
assumptions behind the price, and the follow-up plan if there is no reply.
