# NDA Drafting & Review

> **Not legal advice.** Template output requires review by a qualified lawyer in
> the governing jurisdiction before signing. Include this line in the deliverable.

## Step 1 — Determine the shape

| Question | Why it matters |
|----------|----------------|
| One-way or mutual? | If both sides will share anything, mutual is nearly always correct and easier to agree |
| Who discloses more? | The heavier discloser wants broad definitions and long terms; the receiver wants the opposite |
| What is the purpose? | The permitted-use clause should name it specifically — "evaluating a potential commercial partnership", not "business purposes" |
| Term of the agreement vs term of the obligation | These are different. A 2-year agreement can carry a 5-year confidentiality obligation |
| Governing law | Ask; do not default silently |

## Step 2 — Cover the required clauses

1. **Parties** — full legal entity names and addresses, not trading names.
2. **Definition of Confidential Information** — what is covered, and whether marking is required. Marking requirements are frequently breached in practice; prefer "disclosed in any form" over "must be marked CONFIDENTIAL" unless you can operationally comply.
3. **Standard exclusions** — information that is public, already known, independently developed, or lawfully received from a third party. These four are standard; an NDA lacking them is unreasonably broad.
4. **Permitted use** — narrow, tied to the stated purpose.
5. **Permitted disclosures** — employees/advisors with need-to-know who are bound by equivalent duties.
6. **Compelled disclosure** — required by law or court order, with notice to the discloser where legally permitted.
7. **Standard of care** — typically "reasonable care, no less than used for its own confidential information".
8. **Term and survival** — perpetual for trade secrets is common; a fixed 3–5 years for everything else.
9. **Return or destruction** on request or termination, with a carve-out for routine backups and legal-retention copies.
10. **No license granted** — disclosure creates no IP rights.
11. **No obligation to proceed** — an NDA is not a deal.
12. **Remedies** — injunctive relief, since damages are hard to prove.
13. **Governing law, jurisdiction, entire agreement, assignment, counterparts.**

## Step 3 — Watch the traps

Flag these whenever they appear — they are the clauses that cause trouble later:

- **Non-compete or non-solicit smuggled into an NDA.** Common and often a deal problem. Call it out explicitly.
- **Residuals clause** — lets the receiver use anything remembered without notes. Very favorable to the receiver; a discloser should usually refuse.
- **Perpetual obligations on all information** — unenforceable in some jurisdictions and operationally impossible.
- **One-sided remedies or fee-shifting** in a nominally mutual NDA.
- **Ownership of feedback/ideas** assigned to one party.
- **Definition so broad it captures publicly available information.**

## Step 4 — Verify

- Is every party name a real legal entity, spelled consistently throughout?
- Do the four standard exclusions all appear?
- Is the term stated for both the agreement and the confidentiality obligation?
- If "mutual", is every obligation genuinely reciprocal? Read each clause asking "does this bind both sides?" — asymmetry hides in mutual-looking documents.
- Are there blanks (`[__]`) left unfilled? List every one for the user.

## Step 5 — Deliver

Full draft with placeholders clearly marked, a summary of the choices made and
why, a list of open items needing a human decision, and the disclaimer.
