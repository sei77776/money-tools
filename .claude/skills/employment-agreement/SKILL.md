# Employment & Contractor Agreements

> **Not legal advice.** Employment law is the most jurisdiction-specific area
> covered by this department, and misclassification carries back-tax and penalty
> exposure. A local employment lawyer must review before use. State this clearly.

## Step 1 — Settle classification first

Everything else depends on this, and getting it wrong is the expensive failure mode.

Signals pointing to **employee** rather than contractor:
- The company controls how, when, and where the work is done
- Work is integral to the core business, not a discrete deliverable
- Ongoing, indefinite relationship with set hours
- Company provides tools, equipment, and workspace
- The person cannot subcontract or work for competitors
- Paid by time rather than by deliverable

Substance governs, not the label on the document. If the signals point to
employee, say so directly — do not draft a contractor agreement for what is
plainly employment because that is what was asked for. State the exposure
(back taxes, social insurance, penalties, reclassification claims) and let the
user decide with the facts in hand.

## Step 2 — Draft the agreement

**Both types need:** parties, start date, scope of work/role, compensation and
payment timing, confidentiality, IP assignment, term and termination, governing law.

**Employment adds:** job title and reporting line, working hours and location,
salary/bonus/equity, statutory benefits and leave, probation period, notice
periods (both directions), post-termination obligations, applicable policies.

**Contractor adds:** deliverables and acceptance criteria, milestone/invoicing
schedule, explicit independent-contractor status, responsibility for own taxes
and insurance, right to subcontract (or not), no benefits/no employee status
acknowledgment, and no exclusivity unless truly intended.

## Step 3 — Get IP assignment right

This is the clause most often defective. Required elements:
- Present assignment ("hereby assigns"), not a promise to assign later.
- Covers work product created in the course of the engagement.
- Carves out clearly-listed prior/background IP the person already owns.
- Includes moral-rights waiver where the jurisdiction recognizes them.
- Obliges further assurances (signing later paperwork for registrations).
- For contractors: **an explicit assignment is mandatory**; work-for-hire doctrine does not reliably apply.

Also note jurisdictional limits: several places restrict assignment of inventions
made entirely on the worker's own time with their own resources.

## Step 4 — Flag restrictive covenants

Non-compete, non-solicit (customers and employees), and garden leave. Flag each
with the jurisdictional reality — non-competes are unenforceable or heavily
limited in a growing number of places, and an overbroad clause can be struck
entirely rather than narrowed. Recommend the narrowest scope, duration, and
geography that protects the actual interest.

## Step 5 — Verify

- Is classification addressed explicitly, with reasoning?
- Is the IP assignment a present assignment with a background-IP carve-out?
- Are notice periods reciprocal and lawful for the jurisdiction?
- Is compensation complete — currency, frequency, overtime treatment, expense policy?
- Any unfilled placeholders? List them.

## Step 6 — Deliver

Draft, classification analysis with the reasoning shown, a list of
jurisdiction-specific items a local lawyer must confirm, and the disclaimer.
