# Cash Flow Management

Operational cash timing: getting through the next 60 days without a missed
payroll or a broken supplier relationship.

## Step 1 — Build the near-term cash calendar

List, by date, for the next 8 weeks:
- Opening balance today (actual, from the bank).
- Committed outflows with their due dates.
- Expected inflows with a **confidence** tag: contracted / invoiced / likely / hoped.

Only contracted and invoiced amounts belong in the base plan. Treat "likely" as
upside and say so — planning on hoped-for cash is how a gap becomes a crisis.

Chart the running balance and identify the **trough** — the lowest point and its
date. The trough, not the month-end figure, is what must be solved.

## Step 2 — Prioritize outflows when cash is short

Pay in this order, and explain the reasoning rather than just the ranking:

1. **Payroll and payroll taxes.** Non-negotiable — late payroll has legal consequences and destroys trust irrecoverably. Withheld payroll taxes in particular are frequently subject to personal liability for directors.
2. **Anything securing the business's ability to operate** — rent where eviction is a risk, critical infrastructure, insurance that lapses on non-payment.
3. **Secured debt** where default triggers acceleration or asset seizure.
4. **Suppliers you cannot replace**, especially sole-source inputs.
5. **Tax authorities** — usually willing to arrange a plan if approached *before* default, far less so after.
6. Everything else, longest-standing relationship last to be delayed.

## Step 3 — Close the gap

Levers, roughly in order of speed and least damage:

- **Accelerate inflows** — invoice immediately rather than at month end; offer a small early-payment discount (~2% for net-10); ask the largest customer directly; take deposits on new work.
- **Delay outflows** — negotiate extended terms *proactively*. Calling a supplier before the due date to agree new terms preserves the relationship; going silent past due destroys it. Never simply not pay without a conversation.
- **Cut discretionary spend** — see `expense-audit` for what is actually cuttable.
- **External** — credit line, invoice financing, owner injection. Note the real cost: invoice factoring is often 15–30% annualized. State the cost rather than presenting it as free.

## Step 4 — Verify

- Does opening cash match the actual bank balance today?
- Are payroll **taxes** included, not just net wages? (The most common omission.)
- Are quarterly and annual items (tax, insurance, license renewals) in the window?
- Does the plan still work under the downside case where the largest receivable slips 30 days?

## Step 5 — Deliver

Weekly cash calendar with the trough highlighted, payment priority list with
dates, the gap-closing actions ranked by speed and cost, and a clear statement of
whether the next payroll is covered under both base and downside cases.
