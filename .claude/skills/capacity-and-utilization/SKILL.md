# Capacity & Utilization

Answers two questions that get decided on gut feel and shouldn't be: *can we
take this on?* and *is it time to hire?*

## Step 1 — Compute real available hours

Start from paid hours and subtract reality:

```
paid hours/year
  − holidays, leave, expected sick days
  − admin, internal meetings, training
  − sales, proposals, and unbilled client communication
  − rework not billable to the client
= genuinely available delivery hours
```

A nominal 2,080-hour year typically yields **1,200–1,500** delivery hours. Using
the nominal figure overstates capacity by 30–40%, which is why teams that "have
room" are constantly late. Use timesheet history if it exists; if it doesn't, say
that the figure is an estimate and widen the margin.

## Step 2 — Measure utilization, and know the right target

```
utilization = billable hours / available hours
```

100% is not the goal. A team at 100% has no slack for illness, overrun, or the
next sale, and it will miss dates. Sustainable targets are typically **70–85%**
for delivery staff, lower for anyone also carrying sales or management.

Report per person, not as an average. An average of 75% can hide one person at
100% and another at 50% — a staffing problem the average conceals entirely.

## Step 3 — Answer "can we take this on?"

1. Estimate the new work's hours by skill type, not just in total. Capacity in the wrong skill is not capacity.
2. Lay it against committed work **by week**, not by month. Monthly totals hide the week where three deadlines collide.
3. Check the constraint person — the one skill or individual everything routes through. That person's calendar is the real capacity limit.
4. Add contingency for the overrun rate you actually run at. If jobs typically go 20% over, plan at 20% over.

If it doesn't fit, the options are: move the timeline, subcontract, decline, or
displace other work. **Taking it anyway and hoping is not an option** — it
converts a capacity problem into a delivery failure across every client at once.

## Step 4 — Hire, subcontract, or decline

| | Use when |
|---|---|
| **Hire** | Demand is sustained (not one peak), the work is core, and cash covers 6+ months of loaded cost before the role pays back |
| **Subcontract** | Peak load, a specialist skill, or unproven sustained demand. Faster and reversible; lower margin |
| **Decline / defer** | Capacity is genuinely full and the work isn't strategically important. Declining well preserves the relationship for later |

Before recommending a hire, compute the loaded annual cost (`pricing-and-margin`)
and test it against `cash-runway-forecast`. A hire that shortens runway below a
safe threshold is a different decision than one that doesn't — surface that.

## Step 5 — Verify

- Are available hours derived from actual timesheets, or estimated? Say which.
- Is the analysis weekly, not monthly?
- Is the constraint person or skill identified by name?
- Does the utilization target leave slack, rather than assuming 100%?
- Does the hiring case survive the downside revenue scenario?

## Step 6 — Deliver

Available hours per person, current and forecast utilization by week, whether the
new work fits (with the arithmetic), the constraint, and a recommendation with
the cash implication stated.
