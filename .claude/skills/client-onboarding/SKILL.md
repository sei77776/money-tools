# Client Onboarding

The gap between "signed" and "working" is where most churn is created. This is
a handover process, not a welcome email.

## Step 1 — Transfer context from sales to delivery

Whoever delivers must know what was actually sold. Capture, in writing:

- The signed scope and deliverables (from the proposal, not from memory of the call).
- What was **promised verbally** but not written down. Ask the salesperson directly — this is the single most common source of a client believing they were shorted.
- Why they bought, and what happens to them if it fails.
- Anything already flagged as a risk during the sale.
- Who the stakeholders are, and which one actually judges success.

If sales and delivery are the same person, still write it down. Memory of a call
degrades within days.

## Step 2 — Kickoff

Run it within a week of signing; momentum decays fast. Agenda:

1. Introductions and roles on both sides — including who to contact for what.
2. Restate the scope and outcome, out loud, and get explicit agreement. **Disagreements surface here or in month three; here is far cheaper.**
3. Timeline with client-side dependencies named and owned.
4. Communication plan: channel, meeting cadence, response-time expectation, escalation path.
5. Definition of done, agreed.
6. Immediate next actions with owners and dates.

Send written notes within 24 hours and ask for confirmation. Silence is not agreement — for anything material, ask for an explicit "yes, that's right".

## Step 3 — Access and setup checklist

Almost every delayed project is delayed here. Chase it in the first week:

- Systems, accounts, and permissions needed — listed specifically, with who grants each
- Data, files, brand assets, credentials (via a password manager, **never over email or chat**)
- NDA or DPA executed if not already (`nda-drafting`, `vendor-msa-review`)
- Billing set up: PO number if required, AP contact, invoicing schedule (`invoicing-and-quotes`)
- Shared workspace, folder, or channel created

## Step 4 — Set expectations explicitly

Say the unglamorous things at the start, when they are neutral information rather
than excuses:

- What you need from them, and what happens to the timeline if it is late.
- Turnaround times, working hours, and holiday closures.
- How change requests are handled and priced.
- How many revision rounds are included.

## Step 5 — First 30 days

Deliver something visible in week 1, even small. Early tangible output converts
buyer's remorse into confidence better than any status report.

Schedule a 30-day check-in with one question that matters: *is this going how you
expected?* Ask it early enough that the answer is still actionable.

## Step 6 — Verify

- Is every access item obtained, or blocked with a named owner and date?
- Has the client confirmed the scope in writing?
- Are verbal promises from the sales process resolved — either delivered, or explicitly renegotiated?
- Is the first invoice scheduled and the AP contact known?
- Is the escalation contact on both sides a named person?

## Step 7 — Deliver

Kickoff notes, the access checklist with status, the agreed timeline with
dependencies, and a flagged list of any gap between what was sold and what is
scoped — surfaced now, not at delivery.
